# Format: The 'Will They Notice?' Format

> This format centers on making a subtle change to a person's environment, routine, or a familiar item to see if they will notice. The video captures the setup, the anticipation, and the subject's genuine, unscripted reaction when they encounter the change. The core appeal lies in the suspense and the authentic human payoff.

**Duration:** 45-60 seconds

## When to Use
When you want to create highly shareable, low-effort content that reveals personality and builds a human connection with your audience. Excellent for topics that are relatable to a broad audience within your niche, like office dynamics, common frustrations, or inside jokes.

## Target Audience
Tech creators, software engineers, and corporate professionals who want to create relatable, human-centric content. It's ideal for showing personality, connecting with an audience on a non-technical level, and creating highly shareable videos with minimal production.

**Works well for:** Work-From-Home Life, Office & Corporate Humor, Developer Tools & Setups, Code Reviews & Pair Programming, UX/UI Design Feedback, Startup Culture Pranks

## What Makes It Work
It leverages the power of genuine human reactions, which are inherently engaging and relatable. The format taps into the brain's desire for closure (the setup/payoff loop) and the accessibility of the premise makes it highly shareable, as viewers often send it to their own partners or colleagues.

### Section Details

**1. Hook** (opening, 3-5 seconds)
State the premise clearly and concisely. Explain what you're going to change or 'trick' and who the unsuspecting subject is. This immediately creates an open loop for the viewer (e.g., 'I replaced my senior dev's favorite debugging tool with a knock-off, let's see if he notices').
- *Visual:* Often a selfie-style shot, speaking directly to the camera in a conspiratorial tone.
- *Audio:* Clear voiceover explaining the premise. No music yet to ensure the premise is understood.
- *Text overlay:* Text overlay summarizing the hook: '[Action I'm taking] to see if [Person] notices.'

**2. Build** (middle, 15-25 seconds)
Show the process of setting up the change. Use a montage of quick cuts to show you making the switch or preparing the 'trick'. Interject with short voiceovers or text overlays that build anticipation and raise the stakes (e.g., 'Okay, he's about to start his code review...', 'I even hid the original so he can't switch back easily').
- *Visual:* Quick cuts showing the action of the setup. Can be POV shots or simple static shots. The goal is to show progress and build tension.
- *Audio:* Upbeat, suspenseful, or playful background music can start here. Short voiceover updates add to the narrative.
- *Text overlay:* Short text snippets providing updates: 'He's on his way back...', 'This looks surprisingly legit.'

**3. Payoff** (closing, 25-30 seconds)
Capture the genuine, unscripted reaction of the subject. Let the scene play out naturally. The camera should be positioned to clearly see their face and capture their authentic confusion, questioning, and eventual realization. This is the most important part of the video.
- *Visual:* A static, hidden camera angle is often best to capture a natural reaction without the subject knowing they're being filmed for this purpose.
- *Audio:* Cut the music to focus entirely on the natural dialogue and sounds of the reaction. Let the authentic moment shine.
- *Text overlay:* Use captions to transcribe the dialogue, especially the key moments of confusion or discovery.


## Pacing
The video starts fast with a clear hook, uses a series of quick cuts in the middle to build momentum and anticipation, and then slows down for the payoff to let the authentic human reaction unfold in real-time.

## Hook Mechanics
The hook establishes a clear premise and a promised payoff, creating a psychological 'open loop'. The viewer's brain craves the closure of knowing the outcome ('Did they notice or not?'), compelling them to watch until the very end.

## Visual Style
The style should be intentionally unpolished and authentic, like it was shot on a phone. Avoid professional lighting or cinematic camera moves. The goal is raw authenticity, not perfection, making the reaction feel more genuine.

## Audio Strategy
Relies on a combination of direct-to-camera voiceover for the setup and captured, real-time dialogue for the payoff. Background music is used sparingly in the middle to build tension but is removed during the reaction to emphasize the authenticity of the moment.

## Common Mistakes
Over-producing the video, which makes the reaction feel staged and inauthentic. Another pitfall is having a hook that is too complex or a change that is either too obvious or too obscure for the subject to believably react to.

## Example Topics
- I replaced the 'Merge' button in our pull request UI with a 'YOLO' button in Figma. Let's see if my product manager notices during our design review.
- I changed my Slack status to 'Compiling...' for the entire day to see if anyone on my team questions why it's taking so long.
- I told my junior dev that we measure productivity by lines of code and gave him a 'helpful' script that adds 1000 comments. Let's see how he reacts.
- I swapped my WFH desk chair with a gaming chair right before my 1-on-1 with my manager to see if they say anything about my 'newfound commitment to ergonomics'.
- I replaced the error message in our app's staging environment with 'Error: Have you tried turning it off and on again?' Will the QA tester notice?
