# Format: The Relatable Stereotype

> This format identifies a universally recognized group or archetype within a niche and comedically exaggerates their most predictable behaviors. It works by creating a highly relatable 'I've been there' or 'I know that person' moment for the audience, driving engagement through shared experience and humor.

**Duration:** 10-25 seconds

## When to Use
Use this format to create highly shareable, light-hearted content that strengthens your connection with your audience. It's ideal for demonstrating a deep understanding of your niche's culture and inside jokes.

## Target Audience
Tech and software engineering creators who want to build community by making fun, relatable content about the shared quirks of developer life, corporate culture, and the tech industry at large.

**Works well for:** Developer Archetypes (Senior vs. Junior, Frontend vs. Backend), Corporate Meeting Culture (Daily Standups, Sprint Planning, Retros), Coding & Debugging Humor ('It works on my machine', Legacy Code), Tech Industry Tropes (Startup Life, AI Hype, Recruiter Outreach), Career & Office Life (Interviews, Performance Reviews, WFH vs. Office)

## What Makes It Work
This format combines humor with the powerful psychological principle of relatability and in-group recognition. Viewers feel seen and understood, prompting them to comment 'this is so true' and share it with others who will also get the joke, creating a viral loop.

### Section Details

**1. Hook** (opening, 1-3 seconds)
Immediately establish the context with a clear, bold text overlay that names the stereotype. The visual should show the start of a very normal, recognizable scene.
- *Visual:* Static shot, establishing the character and environment.
- *Audio:* Ambient sound or the beginning of dialogue.
- *Text overlay:* '[Demographic] when they [Situation]' or '[Type of Person] every time they [Action]'. Example: 'Senior Devs when a Junior finds a bug in their code'.

**2. Setup** (middle, 5-15 seconds)
Act out the universally recognizable part of the stereotype. This is the 'before' state, where the behavior is predictable but not yet exaggerated. This builds anticipation for the punchline.
- *Visual:* Simple, continuous shot focusing on the character's dialogue and actions.
- *Audio:* Natural dialogue that sets up the premise of the stereotype.

**3. Payoff** (closing, 3-7 seconds)
Cut abruptly to the exaggerated, ridiculous outcome of the stereotype. Deliver the punchline and end the video *immediately* after. Do not add an outro or let the scene linger, as the sharp cut maximizes comedic timing and watch time.
- *Visual:* A hard cut, or a transition card (e.g., '2 hours later...', 'After one code review...'). The final shot should be a strong visual punchline.
- *Audio:* Deliver the final line of dialogue or use a sound effect that emphasizes the ridiculous outcome.


## Pacing
The pacing starts at a normal speed during the setup to feel authentic, then accelerates to an abrupt, sudden ending with the payoff. The transition from setup to payoff should be jarring and quick.

## Hook Mechanics
The hook is a direct text overlay that explicitly states the stereotype being shown. It works by giving the audience complete context instantly, creating an 'in-joke' and making them want to watch to see the familiar scenario play out in a funny way.

## Visual Style
The visual style is typically simple, often self-shot, and relies on performance over production value. A clear visual contrast between the 'setup' scene and the 'payoff' scene is key.

## Audio Strategy
Relies on original audio and dialogue to deliver the comedy. Music is secondary and should not overpower the performance. Sound effects can be used to punctuate the final payoff.

## Common Mistakes
Failing to end the video immediately after the payoff. Adding an unnecessary outro or lingering on the final shot kills the comedic timing and reduces viewer retention. Another mistake is choosing a stereotype that is too obscure or not widely recognized by the target audience.

## Example Topics
- Senior engineers asked to look at a junior's 'quick question' at 4:55 PM on a Friday.
- A developer after saying 'I'll just push this one last change before the weekend'.
- Product managers adding 'just one more small thing' to the sprint.
- How backend developers see the frontend.
- Me telling myself I'll work on my side project this weekend.
